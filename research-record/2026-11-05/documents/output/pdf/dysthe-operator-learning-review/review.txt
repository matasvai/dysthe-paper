          Learning Dysthe Evolution Across Initial Wave Fields
             A literature-grounded numerical, FNO, and PINN research protocol



                                           25 October 2026


                                                Abstract
          The proposed goal is to learn the evolution of a bounded family of complex water-wave
      envelopes and establish how far predictions remain accurate on unseen initial fields. Begin
      with the approved spatial free-surface Dysthe equation, a qualified Fourier reference, and a
      short-distance Fourier neural operator (FNO). Compare the same architecture with and without
      a full equation residual; use an ordinary physics-informed neural network (PINN) on a small
      set of individual problems to diagnose physics training. A function-conditioned PINN is an
      optional operator comparison. Success means accurate modulation growth, focusing, recurrence
      and spectral transfer at a measured cost and within a declared validity range. It does not mean
      all-distance accuracy or a proof of global smoothness. This report reviews primary literature,
      audits the current implementation, specifies data and acceptance gates, and supplies small
      independent operator checks. No FNO/PINN was trained and no new long-distance evolution
      campaign was run for this review.


1    The question and the proposed contribution
      For a specified family of smooth initial free-surface envelopes, can a learned solution
      operator predict unseen wave fields and their modulation events over longer propagation
      distances, with controlled error and useful amortized cost relative to a tuned numerical
      solver?

    This question has three separately measurable parts. First, learn a map across initial fields, rather
than fitting a single trajectory. Second, determine the distance over which repeated predictions
remain qualified. Third, test whether the method retains the particular water-wave effects for
which Dysthe corrections are useful. Reproducing known modulation phenomena is validation; a
reproducible improvement in generalization, accuracy per unit cost, or reliable prediction distance
would be the methodological contribution. Novel wave physics is not assumed.
    The first comparison should be a supervised FNO and the same distance-conditioned FNO with
a physics loss, commonly called a physics-informed neural operator (PINO). The FNO and PINO
literature supports these method classes, but does not establish their performance on this Dysthe
problem [10, 11]. An ordinary PINN trained for each initial field answers a different question and
incurs new optimization cost for every field. A branch-conditioned physics-informed operator can
answer the multi-input question [12, 13].
    Water-wave studies provide closer precedents. Ehlers et al. fit a separate complex-output NLS
PINN to synthetic sparse-gauge data, with coefficient identification for model mismatch; Dysthe
appears as future work [20]. Ehlers, Stender and Hoffmann use an FNO-based PINO with high-
order-spectral free-surface residuals to reconstruct phase-resolved surfaces across sensor inputs

[21]. Eeltink et al. combine viscous MNLS with LSTM corrections learned from breaking-wave
experiments; their magnitude and spectral-magnitude targets and wave-type-dependent corrections
do not validate complex inviscid rollouts [22]. These precedents motivate distinguishing instance
fitting from operator generalization, fixed-equation approximation from missing-physics correction,
and reconstruction from autonomous propagation. This is a focused methods review, not a claim of
first use or an exhaustive priority search.
    Three proposed hypotheses make negative results useful:
    1. A short-distance FNO generalizes within a bounded initial-data distribution at a declared
       error tolerance.
    2. Adding a correctly evaluated residual improves held-out rollout accuracy or reduces the amount
       of reference data required, after accounting for its extra cost.
    3. Short rollout training extends the reliable prediction distance without suppressing true
       modulational instability.
Failure of any hypothesis is a legitimate result. None implies a statement about existence of the
underlying PDE.

2.1    How the two supplied papers enter this project
Dysthe’s 1979 paper derives a temporal potential-envelope correction and studies sideband instability
[1]. It motivates the mean-flow term and a modulation-growth test. Its variables, dimensionality
and coefficients cannot be pasted into (2.1). The supplied Schober–Islas preprint develops a weakly
viscous hierarchy and a separate finite-adjustment-time mean-flow closure [2]. It motivates a future,
separately specified dissipative study. It neither supplies an operator-learning algorithm nor proves
continued smoothness for the present spatial equation.
    The earlier companion review, Dysthe Evolution: Numerical Evidence and Existence (23 Oc-
tober 2026), records two apparent linear-dispersion inconsistencies in the supplied version 1 and
distinguishes its real relaxation closure from a complex damping coefficient. Those issues must be
resolved before implementing that extension. The present plan keeps the inviscid spatial model
fixed; it does not silently introduce viscosity, transverse coordinates, or a different envelope variable.

2.2    What currently exists
The five active repositories agree on the model and case schema. The local core supplies complex128
Fourier differentiation, projected nonlinear products, integrating-factor RK4, and diagnostics. It
keeps |m| < N/4, which provides room for its cubic products. The learning registry still marks all
methods unimplemented: there is no active water-wave FNO/PINN training pipeline.
   Stored short runs at ϵ = .025, L = 160, N = 4096, h = .00125, Ξ = 4 include profile, perturbed-
profile and Gaussian qualification records. Their largest stored raw relative L2 grid discrepancy
is about 4.2 × 10−15 , step discrepancy 4.1 × 10−9 , and domain discrepancy 2.2 × 10−5 . These are
historical two-level comparisons for those cases, not fresh trajectories, error bounds, or evidence
for arbitrary inputs. The Gaussian and solitary archive hashes were freshly checked against their
reports; the core source hash agrees. A historical Gaussian runner hash differs from today’s runner
after later formula changes.
   A research dataset needs a new bounded generator using the core. The demonstration runner fixes
ϵ = .025, Ξ ≤ 4, and at most 101 output stations; part of its initializer also hardcodes that epsilon
in provenance. Do not expand the meaning of those records to cover a new parameter campaign.
Before packaging a reproducible run, align the learning repository’s pinned core dependency with
the actual reviewed reference implementation.

Store reference fields as complex128 arrays [Nξ , Nτ ] with τj = −L/2+jL/N , excluding the duplicated
endpoint. Store explicit propagation coordinates. The existing runner’s rounded output indices can
create unequal increments; validate the actual spacing or condition the operator on each actual
increment.
    Assign one immutable split group to each base initial draw. All its windows, refinements,
translated or phase-rotated copies, paired disturbances, and coefficient variants remain in the same
split. Test for duplicate field hashes as well as mislabeled groups. Generate parameter ranges and
seed lists before fitting. Fit global normalization on training fields only. Use validation for model
selection; open the final test set once the protocol and checkpoint are frozen. Snapshots from one
field are correlated samples, not independent cases.
    The current metadata validator is insufficient by itself. The new loader must check actual shapes,
dtypes, finite values, coordinates, hashes, model ID, epsilon, grid origin, and qualification status.

the intended surrogate error budget. Choose the number of learned Fourier modes through a small
validation sweep, such as 32, 64, 128, after inspecting the physical spectra. These numbers do not
specify the FNO output bandwidth.

4     Implementations that answer the comparison
The first comparison should change the training objective while keeping the representation fixed.
Use ϵ = .025 and L = 160, and let Fϵ denote the right-hand side of (2.1). An ordinary PINN
approximates one solution after its initial condition has been fixed. An operator accepts an initial
function and predicts its evolution. Training a coordinate network on several different initial fields
without an input identifying the field does not define that operator.
   The original FNO learns through Fourier multipliers, pointwise channel maps and nonlinearities;
its demonstrations include Burgers, Darcy and Navier–Stokes [10, §§3–5]. PINO adds physics
objectives and distinguishes operator training from later optimization for one input [11, §3]. The
construction below is our proposed adaptation to spatial water-wave Dysthe. Neither paper
establishes its performance for this equation.

4.2   A full residual requires a whole profile
For a differentiable reconstructed output v = Gθ (u, ϵ, s), define

                                        rθ (u, s) = ∂s v − Fϵ (v).                                 (4.2)

At each sampled s, evaluate a complete periodic slice. Compute ρ = |v|2 and |Dτ |ρ using the
multiplier |km |, with zero mean mode, and form all derivative nonlinearities. Sparse independent
points in (s, τ ) cannot determine this nonlocal term. Automatic differentiation through the Fourier
evaluation allows training, but does not remove the need to resolve the profile quadrature.
   Declare the continuum reconstruction of grid outputs. A convenient choice is an explicit final
Fourier projection to |m| ≤ Kout , with the input represented in the same space. Report its initial
projection error and refine Kout . This final bandwidth is distinct from the number of learned modes
in each FNO layer: skip paths and pointwise nonlinearities can generate higher frequencies [11, §3
and Appendix E][15, §§3–4]. For this explicitly truncated output, the cubic right-hand side can
reach 3Kout . A uniform quadrature with Q > 6Kout resolves the full residual and its squared spatial
L2 norm, up to floating-point error. Retain these discarded nonlinear modes in the loss; projecting
the residual back to the output band tests only a Galerkin system. This bound does not de-alias
arbitrary internal neural nonlinearities. Refine the network evaluation grid separately.
   For an endpoint-only FNO, a finite difference or an RK defect in distance measures a discrete
update. It is not (4.2) without a specified differentiable reconstruction. Equation (4.1) supplies that
distance dependence; Fourier differentiation then refers to the declared profile reconstruction, not
an unspecified off-grid network.

4.4    Where a PINN or DeepONet adds information
Use an ordinary PINN first as a per-input diagnostic: on one short slab, represent vθ (s, τ ) periodically,
enforce its initial value, and minimize the full residual. Its training cost is incurred for each input.
A sequential PINN carries its learned endpoint into the next slab; supplying a reference endpoint
instead changes the experiment. Published failure studies support testing short intervals and explicit
optimization controls [17, §5]. Causal residual weighting prioritizes earlier parts of an interval during
training [16, §3]; its weights are optimization diagnostics, not solution-error certificates. These
findings motivate the proposed slab strategy but do not guarantee it for Dysthe.

can test another representation. An encoder receives the initial Fourier coefficients; a smooth

5     Which long-distance behavior is worth predicting?
The scientific target is the evolution of a complex wave envelope, including its phase, modulation and
transfer between Fourier modes. A useful learned model should preserve these features for unseen
initial packets over a declared propagation interval. Agreement with the same Dysthe equation tests
the surrogate; agreement with measured water waves tests the physical model. These are separate
comparisons.

5.1   Modulation, focusing and recurrence
Dysthe’s 1979 calculation identifies induced mean flow as a central correction to modulation-
instability predictions [1]. It motivates measuring early sideband growth and the onset of nonlinear
exchange. Its temporal potential-envelope formulas must not be substituted directly into the
present spatial free-surface equation. Instead, use the linearization of (2.1) and compare small
perturbations of a carrier at several modulation frequencies, amplitudes and relative phases. A
growing perturbation is evidence of instability of that carrier, not evidence of wave breaking or loss
of smoothness.
    Localized packets offer a complementary test. Lo and Mei studied asymmetric leaning and splitting,
together with unequal sideband growth, using a higher-order envelope model and comparisons with
experiments [4]. For Gaussian and sech-like initial packets, measure maximum amplification, the
distance and profile location of that maximum, packet asymmetry and spectral broadening. Varying
width, chirp and amplitude tests distinct interactions between dispersion and nonlinearity. A small
average field error can still hide an incorrect focusing distance or an underestimated peak, so these
observables require separate error measures.
    Recurrence tests a longer sequence of transfers from carrier to sidebands and back. Armaroli,
Brunetti and Kasparian compare full surface-envelope HONLS evolution with a three-mode reduction
and distinguish recurrence classes through relative phase [5]. Their reduction is useful as a
cheap comparator only while omitted modes remain small; their normalization requires an explicit
conversion before quantitative reuse. Measure carrier depletion, sideband imbalance, recurrence
distance and the complex field at return. A recurrence defect may minimize over a constant phase
and a profile translation, but both fitted shifts and the unaligned error should also be reported.
Repeated maxima alone do not establish a breather or accurate recurrence.
    Two-packet interactions provide an optional harder test. Fedele and Dutykh numerically observe
radiation and changed profiles after spatial-Dysthe collisions, contrasting with their NLS control
[3]. Compare the joint evolution with separately propagated packets and quantify outgoing shape,
phase, displacement and radiation. The comparison must resolve the small interaction signal and
account for overlap; a subtraction artifact is not emitted radiation, and a collision calculation is not
a proof of nonintegrability.

chirp can broaden the spectrum. The carrier remains a deep-water wave in this model. Moreover,

X when steepness varies.

   Physical long-distance comparisons need further care. Carter and Govan derive a viscous Dysthe
model and compare downshift observables with tank data [7]. A later comparison across four
experiments finds that model performance depends on the observable and initial sideband seeding;
no model captures every measured evolution [8]. Transverse modulation can also transfer energy
and affect downshift in a wide tank [9]. The present inviscid, one-profile equation does not represent
dissipation, transverse evolution or breaking.
   Finally, accurate finite-distance fields and bounded sampled derivatives support a resolved
computational observation. They do not establish global smooth existence. A small training
residual does not establish that either: causal PINN training addresses optimization and information
propagation [16], while existence and uniform error certification require additional mathematical
estimates.

Goal. Eliminate sign, phase, nonlocal-operator and data-interface errors before interpreting a

6.3   T1. Qualify the numerical reference and representation
Goal. Establish that the target trajectories and learning grid resolve the quantities the research
intends to predict.

Experiment and control. Use representative low/high amplitudes, narrow/wide packets, strong
chirps and unstable sidebands. Compare N, 2N, 4N at fixed L, and h, h/2, h/4 on a sufficiently
fine grid. Check expected RK4 step convergence before roundoff dominates. For localized packets
compare L, 2L and, if necessary, 4L at fixed spacing, reconstructing the same localized field instead
of periodically repeating it. Compare common windows, derivative tails and mean flow. Perform
the learning-grid projection check separately. Fourier methods have direct spatial Dysthe precedent
[3, 4]; the specific gates here are proposed.

Decision and limitation. Accept only cases passing the common reference budget and spectral
checks. A larger domain can alter nonlocal mean flow even when edge amplitudes are tiny. An
unresolved case triggers refinement or a shorter qualified distance, not a claim of singularity.
Full-period wavetrains have a different boundary question from localized packets.

6.4    T2. Learn unseen initial fields at fixed parameters
Goal. Determine whether the FNO learns the declared short-distance operator rather than
memorizing a small collection of trajectories.

Experiment and control. Train on the three-family group split at fixed epsilon and length.
Evaluate all held-out profiles without retraining. Compare one-step and autonomous rollout errors
to a coarser spectral solver whose step and grid are tuned on validation for the same accuracy.
Include the exact linear propagator and independently resolved NLS evolution as physical controls,
while recognizing that neither solves the full Dysthe problem. Use nested training subsets to produce
a learning curve [10].

Decision and limitation. Pass only if the prespecified held-out errors and failure fraction meet
the goal. A conservative first main-campaign target is at least 90% of test groups passing the
primary gates through Ξ = 4, with uncertainty and all failures reported. This establishes bounded-
distribution interpolation only. If a cheap spectral solve dominates accuracy and cost, report that
outcome before adding model complexity.

6.5    T3. Does the full physics loss actually help?
Goal. Isolate the benefit, if any, of physics training for this equation and input population.

Experiment and control. Compare the same initialized FNO backbone, data, hard initial
condition, linear skip, rollout schedule and validation protocol with λr = 0 and λr > 0. Select loss
weights using validation only. Report both a matched-compute comparison and the extra cost to
reach a matched accuracy; physics updates cost more. Evaluate on new collocation stations and
refined full-profile residuals. At a fixed architecture, repeat on two or three data budgets to test
data efficiency [11].

Decision and limitation. Physics is useful if it improves the intended held-out metric or needs
less reference data at acceptable total cost. A lower training residual alone fails this decision.
A physics-only branch is optional after the hybrid works; it is not necessary to launch a large
architecture search. If benefits disappear under independent residual refinement, investigate aliasing
first.

6.6    T4. What can a PINN contribute?
Goal. Separate single-problem optimization from genuine multi-input physics-informed prediction.

Experiment and control. Choose six qualified validation cases, two per family. For an ordinary
Fourier PINN, compare a single interval, sequential distance slabs, and slabs with causal residual
weighting at comparable optimization budgets [16, 17]. Carry predicted endpoints forward; do not
reset to exact interior snapshots. If this reveals a useful training strategy, test a function-conditioned
Fourier/DeepONet model on unseen initial fields with no optimization [13].

Decision and limitation. Count per-case optimization, failed restarts and all slabs. A PINN can
be informative without beating the spectral solver. The six-case exercise is a training diagnostic, not
a broad statistical comparison. A parameter-conditioned network only generalizes over its declared

set.

6.7   T5. How far does autonomous prediction remain useful?
Goal. Measure a defensible accuracy horizon and whether rollout training extends it.

Experiment and control. Train on reference intervals no longer than Ξ = 4 and repeat learned
local maps on their own outputs to Ξ = 8, 16, then conditionally 32. Compare one-step training,
a short multi-step rollout loss, and optionally stopped-gradient pushforward training [14]. Use
validation to select the rollout depth before testing. Plot error and the qualified distance per held-out
group. Inspect spectral-error growth, not only norm drift [15].

Decision and limitation. Claim an extension only for the fixed test population and tolerances.
Never reset the rollout with a reference state. For a locally Lipschitz exact map the conditional

6.8   T6. Preserve real modulational instability
Goal. Determine whether a learned method reproduces physical sideband amplification instead of
damping it away.

Experiment and control. For amplitudes and modulation modes covering stable and unstable
regions, compare the full signed perturbation response with exp(ξM (k)) from Appendix A, a refined
Dysthe trajectory, and the NLS limit. Halve the perturbation until the early response converges to
the linear prediction while remaining above numerical noise. Check both sideband amplitudes, their
phases and growth rates. The observable is motivated by Dysthe’s instability calculation [1].

Decision and limitation. Use a provisional 5% gain/growth tolerance over the verified linear
interval, with absolute tolerances near zero growth. Require the perturbation signal to exceed the
reference error by a declared margin. A filter that keeps a rollout smooth but suppresses the correct
growing mode fails. Linear amplification is neither nonlinear blow-up nor wave breaking.

6.9   T7. Predict focusing and recurrence events
Goal. Test the wave behavior that matters beyond average field error.

Experiment and control. For localized packets measure the first focus distance, amplification,
peak trajectory and asymmetry against refined Dysthe; compare NLS to identify the size of the
higher-order effect [4]. For periodic modulations measure carrier depletion, sideband exchange
and recurrence distance [5]. Record raw complex recurrence defect and separately minimize over
translation and constant phase. If no event occurs in the qualified reference interval, label it
absent/censored rather than forcing a fit.

Decision and limitation. Prespecify event definitions and the 5% primary event target. Similar
peak oscillations do not establish recurrence of the complex wave field or a stable breather. Track
spectral mean and peak separately and test the exact centroid identity (5.1). Two-packet collisions,
with separated single-packet controls and a larger-domain check, are a later family-transfer test;

6.10    T8. State exactly which generalization is being tested
Goal. Find the boundary of the learned map’s competence.

Experiment and control. Change one factor at a time: withheld packet family; new epsilon
values within the training interval; epsilon outside it; finer grids on the same domain; a different
domain length; longer propagation. Each receives a separate score and qualification. If testing
measurement noise later, specify the observation and reconstruction processes and compare to an
appropriate estimator, not to a solver supplied with inaccessible noiseless phase.

Decision and limitation. An interpolation result does not imply extrapolation. A finer output
grid does not recover missing input frequencies. A domain change alters physical wavenumbers and
periodic mean flow. Do not pool these tasks into one favorable average or declare arbitrary-input
performance.

6.11    T9. Is there an amortized computational benefit?
Goal. Determine whether the learned solver saves work for the intended repeated-query workload.

Experiment and control. Compare against the fastest numerical configuration that passes the
same field, derivative and event gates. Measure hardware, precision, batch size, peak memory,
output density, warm-up, inference, data generation, validation, all training seeds and any test-time
optimization. Report accuracy–cost curves, not one speed ratio. For a fixed workload a useful
accounting identity is

Decision and limitation. The estimated break-even query count is meaningful only at matched
accuracy and for the specified hardware/batching regime. If the denominator is nonpositive, there

7      Implementation order and what this review verifies
First deliverable: a reproducible numerical population. Add a separate research generator,
array-aware validation, immutable initial-group manifests and spectral coarsening. Store source/array
hashes, the exact model ID, epsilon, coordinates, generation seeds and qualification records. Exercise
an interrupted/resumed small generation run. Existing data can supply controls, but cannot
substitute for the new distribution or a frozen test split.


                                                 15
Second deliverable: one honest operator comparison. Add the differentiable RHS adapter
and a distance-conditioned FNO. Use a small declared architecture, for example four Fourier blocks
of width 32 or 64 with smooth activation; choose only a few mode/width settings on validation. Start
with the 48-group pilot and the supervised objective. Add the full residual to the same backbone
and verify the six ordinary PINN diagnostic cases. Store best and last checkpoints, optimizer state,
random-generator state, normalization, training groups and code versions. Check that resumed
optimization reproduces the uninterrupted run within the declared determinism tolerance. Never
select the best test seed or hide failed runs.

Third deliverable: a qualified behavior comparison. Only after the pilot passes, enlarge
the dataset and evaluate the locked long-distance panel. Publish per-case errors, event definitions,
unresolved cases, horizon limits and matched-accuracy costs. A useful result may be that physics
improves data efficiency but adds inference-independent training cost, that rollout training helps
only a subset of inputs, or that the spectral solver remains the better computational choice. The
experiment is designed to distinguish these outcomes rather than presume a neural advantage.

Existence remains a separate mathematical task. The relevant computational question is
whether the selected initial data continue to exhibit resolved bounded derivatives over the tested
distance. An all-distance smoothness claim needs a local theory and continuation criterion for
this exact formulation, followed by the necessary uniform bounds, or an appropriate validated a
posteriori argument for a specified finite interval. Temporal whole-space Dysthe theory [18] does
not automatically apply to this periodic spatial equation. The residual must be combined with the
relevant stability, regularity and quadrature estimates [19]; independent training and agreement of
two solvers do not supply those estimates. A theorem project should begin with one trajectory and
one short interval after the numerical pipeline is qualified.
