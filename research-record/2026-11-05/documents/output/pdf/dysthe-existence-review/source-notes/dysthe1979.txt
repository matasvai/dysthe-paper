      The ordinary nonlinear Schrodinger equation for deep water waves,
      found by perturbation analysis to 0(63) in the wave-steepness e = ka, is
      shown to compare rather unfavourably with the exact calculations of
      Longuet-Higgins (I978b) for e > 0.15, say.
           We show that a significant improvement can be achieved by taking the
      perturbation analysis one step further 0(64). The dominant new effect
      introduced to order 64 iS the mean flow response to non-uniformities in the
      radiation stress caused by modulation of a finite amplitude wave.



                                            1. INTRODUCTION
Following the papers by Lighthill (I965, I967), Whitham (I967), Benjamin &
Feir (I967), Benjamin (I967), and Zakharov (I967) there has been considerable
interest in the stability of finite amplitude gravity waves on deep water. A lot of
attention has been paid to the instability of a wave train to modulational pertur-
bations.
   With the prominent exception of the recent papers by Longuet-Higgins (I 978 a, b),
which used the exact equations, most authors are using approximate model equa-
tions in analysing the stability. For large amplitudes and perturbations of very long
wavelengths, Whitham's method gives the best result, as shown by Lighthill (i965,
I967), and confirmed by the exact results of Longuet-Higgins (I978 b). The most
severe limitation on this method is the requirement that the wavelength of the
perturbation be very long, which is not true for the most rapidly growing mode when
the wave steepness ka exceeds 0.1 (say).
   For small amplitudes, the most successful and elegant approach is through the
application of the so-called nonlinear Schrodinger equation (hereafter denoted
n.l.S.). This was done by Zakharov (I968), who allowed for the finite amplitude
wavetrain to be subjected to modulational perturbations in two horizontal dimen-
sions (both along and perpendicular to the direction of the wavetrain). Later, Davey
 & Stewartson (I 974) extended this to waves on finite depth. The connection between

Whitham's method and the n.l.S. approach was investigated by Dysthe (i974) and

  While the n.l.S. approach is valid for smaller perturbation wavelengths than
Whitham's method, the most severe restriction here is the smallness of the amplitude.
  For small amplitudes, ka < 0.1 say, the n.l.S. predictions, when compared to
Longuet-Higgins's exact results, are fairly accurate. It has also been shown experi-
mentally by Yuen & Lake (I975) that the n.l.S. provides a quantitatively satis-
factory description of the long-time evolution of wave packets for small amplitudes.
  Comparing the predictions of the n.l.S. in the parametric range 0.15 < ka with
Longuet-Higgins's results, shows the short-comings of the n.l.S. rather clearly (e.g.
at ka = 0.3 the maximum growth rate is overestimated by a factor of 2). There seems
to be a case for an improved model equation, which can cover more of the parameter
range 0.15 < ka < 0.42, between that of the n.l.S., and up to the theoretical limit
ka = 0.42 where the fast growing breaking instability starts (see Longuet-Higgins
I978).
  The aim of the present paper is to investigate more closely the reason for the
rather limited range of validity of the n.l.S. equation, and to find ways of improving
it. This is achieved by taking the perturbation expansion in the small parameter
e = ka one step further than is necessary in the derivation of the n.l.S. equation.
This means including a number of terms in the evolution equation for the amplitude
of the wavetrain, as well as taking into account the small mean flow brought about
by the radiation stresses of the modulated wavetrain (see Longuet-Higgins &
Stewart I964, Stewartson I977).
  As it turns out, the most significant contribution comes from the induced mean
flow, whose component in the direction of propagation of the wave causes a local
Doppler shift. This frequency shift then gives rise to changes in the stability proper-
ties of the wavetrain.
   In the two dimensional case, the improved equation removes a rather unphysical
feature of the n.l.S. equation: namely its prediction that the maximum growth rate
of the modulational instability occurs when the wavevector K = {A, /t} of the

The physical picture of the influence of the added term and equations is rather clear:
   (i) Modulation of the finite amplitude wavetrain causes non-uniformity of the
radiation-stress.
   (ii) The resulting forces bring about a mean flow response of the fluid (which is
still a potential flow, since it is caused by a transient rather than a dissipative
mechanism).
   (iii) The horizontal component of this mean flow along the direction of wave
propagation causes a slowly varying Doppler shift of the wave as represented by the
last term in (4.1).
   (iv) The Doppler shift then has a 'detuning 'effectonthemodulational instability.
